AIGER KITCHEN / UX & PRODUCT DESIGN / 2025–2026

Start cooking.
The hood starts with you.

Cooking should not depend on remembering every switch. I designed a connected-kitchen concept that links ventilation to ignition, while keeping appliance status and manual controls visible.

01 / THE PROBLEM

The hood was an extra step
people remembered too late.

13 / 22

Survey respondents remembered the extractor hood only after switching on the stove.

Question 6 · 59.09% · Multiple selections allowed

Design around the action they already take.

The survey made a specific sequence visible: stove first, hood later. That gave the interaction a starting point—ignition—rather than another step for the cook to remember.

20 interviews focused on middle-income adults aged 55–65. The 22-response questionnaire included a broader age range. This finding is self-reported survey evidence, not a measured failure rate.

02 / FROM EVIDENCE TO DESIGN

Use the stove action.
Make the response understandable.

REPORTED ROUTINE

Turn on stove → remember hood → turn on hood

A second appliance depends on a separate remembered action.

PROPOSED INTERACTION

Ignite stove → hood activates → review shared status

The concept removes the separate hood-on step from this sequence. Individual controls remain available.

01 / TRIGGER

Begin with ignition.

Placing a pan prepares the sequence. Ignition starts ventilation and lighting.

02 / FEEDBACK

Show what changed.

A shared appliance overview makes the cooktop and hood states visible together.

03 / CONTROL

Keep an individual choice.

Automatic coordination does not remove the hood's manual control.

Design rationale: a reminder would still leave a second action to complete. Linking the response addresses that step directly; whether users prefer this behavior still needs evaluation.

04 / INTERFACE ITERATION

Make the response easier to find.

The current browser iteration brings device state and action together. These are interface changes, not measured usability gains.

From separate actions to a shared state

The reported routine required remembering the hood separately. The proposed flow links it to ignition and displays both devices together.

From a broad system to two destinations

The current interface groups everyday device controls under Appliances and urgent situations under Safety & assistance.

What changed—and what is established

The prototype now demonstrates ignition activating ventilation, visible device states and manual override. The original project included simulated control-interface sessions with 10 participants. This browser iteration has not been retested; reduced forgetting, faster task completion and safety improvements have not been measured.

Research context & next evaluation

Other survey findings: 18/22 worried about forgetting to switch off the stove or appliances; 11/22 returned to check equipment after leaving the kitchen. These informed the wider status and assistance concepts.

A follow-up evaluation should ask users to start cooking, explain which devices responded and why, and override the hood. Task completion, missed feedback and unintended actions would help assess whether the design is clearer.

Sample: 19 questionnaire respondents were 55–65, one was 71–75, and two were over 75. Multiple selections were allowed. Survey counts describe reported routines and concerns, not prototype outcomes.
